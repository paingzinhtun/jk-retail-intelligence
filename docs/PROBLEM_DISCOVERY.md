# Problem discovery

## Target user

Owner or manager of a traditional small or medium retailer in Myanmar that records purchases manually and does not consistently capture item-level sales and stock.

## Current process

Supplier → purchase → receive → display/store → sell → collect cash → inspect shelves → reorder from memory.

## Assumed recurring problems to validate with real users

| Problem | Operational cause | Likely consequence | Decision affected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product-level profit is unknown | Sales and cost are not joined by SKU | Cash is allocated without margin visibility | Which products deserve capital? |
| Frequent stockouts | No current-stock estimate or reorder rule | Lost sales and weaker customer trust | What should be purchased now? |
| Purchasing depends on memory | No demand summary | Overbuying slow items and underbuying fast items | How much should be ordered? |
| Best sellers are unclear | No item-level aggregation | Shelf space and attention may be misallocated | Which products matter most? |
| Reports take too long | Handwritten records require reconstruction | Decisions are delayed and uncertain | What happened in the last cycle? |

## Hypothesis

If a retailer records a manageable group of 20–50 products reliably, a five-day report can reduce reporting time and reveal stock and purchasing exceptions that memory alone misses.

## Questions for real interviews

1. Tell me about the last time an important product ran out.
2. How did you decide what to purchase during the last five-day cycle?
3. Which product decisions create the most uncertainty?
4. How long does it take to estimate sales and profit?
5. Which records are missing or difficult to maintain?
6. What happens when a purchasing decision is wrong?
7. Who would use a five-day report, and what action would they take?

## Pilot success criteria

- Track 20–50 products for at least 10 operating days.
- Achieve at least 90% completeness for required fields.
- Produce the five-day report in under five minutes after data entry is complete.
- Identify low-stock products without examining every shelf.
- Obtain evidence that at least one recommendation affected a real decision.

The statements above are hypotheses until verified through observation and interviews.
